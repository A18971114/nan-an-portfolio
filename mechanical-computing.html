Mechanical Reservoir Computing through Topology Optimization

Graduate research participant · Carnegie Mellon University · Prof. Wang

Back to projects

Introduction and Objective

A mechanical structure can retain and transform information through its dynamic response. Designing this response could allow future physical systems to combine mechanical function with computation. This project investigates how mass–spring network topology affects memory and nonlinear processing, with the goal of identifying reservoirs with useful computational characteristics.

Design and My Contribution

I developed a configurable network generator, integrated Taichi simulation into the evaluation workflow, and implemented a genetic algorithm using DEAP. Candidate networks varied in mass distribution, connection type, and connection deletion. Their simulated responses were evaluated using R²-based task-performance maps. I then implemented MAP-Elites, a quality-diversity method, to organize candidates into four behavioral regions defined by memory and nonlinearity.

Results and Evaluation

Baseline comparisons in the research log supported the usefulness of genetic-algorithm optimization. In a subsequent 300-evaluation MAP-Elites run, archive coverage and quality showed little improvement after approximately 150 evaluations, and one behavioral region remained unfilled. The observed networks suggested that strong nonlinearity was more selective than memory and that the two capacities did not follow a strict trade-off. These findings are conditional on the tested search space, input settings, and classification thresholds.

Conclusion

The study produced a simulation-based search and comparison framework for a future mechanical reservoir library. Search saturation and sensitivity to input placement motivate broader sampling and testing across input conditions before general design rules or physical performance can be established.
